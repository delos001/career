# CV / Resume Best Practices Research (Senior Leadership and Senior IC, Regulated Industries)

**Used by:** cv-targeted (career-strategist grounding); evidence base behind `cv-structure.md`.

Refresh per the `cv-best-practices-refresh` deferral. The cv-targeted skill warns
when `last_researched` is over ~12 months old (the AI-tell guidance ages fastest).

## Overall Summary

The strongest evidence supports a reverse-chronological structure with experience leading the document, impact-first quantified bullets, and tailoring to the job description; these are well-corroborated across academic studies and reputable practitioner sources. The most consequential correction to popular belief is that ATS systems rarely auto-reject resumes: the viral "75% of resumes are auto-rejected and never seen by a human" statistic is fabricated (traceable to a defunct vendor, Preptel, c. 2012, with no methodology). The real screen-out problem is human recruiters under volume plus over-prescriptive criteria that recruiters and hiring managers configure (Harvard/Accenture). Two specific empirical findings are worth building toward: a Nature Human Behaviour field experiment (N=9,022) showing that listing tenure as "years worked" beats date ranges, and the ResumeGo simulation showing recruiters prefer two-page resumes, with that preference rising with seniority.

## Overall Evidence Quality and Limitations

- True peer-reviewed empirical work on resume content/format is thin. The clearest exception is Kristal et al. (2022, Nature Human Behaviour) on employment-gap formatting.
- Much of the quantitative "callback lift" data (tailoring percentages, keyword-match rates) comes from career-tech vendors (Jobscan, ResumeGo, Ladders, Enhancv, Scale.jobs) whose methodologies are partial, self-serving, or unpublished. These are treated here as [low-confidence] or directional, not established, unless independently corroborated.
- Several widely cited numbers are circular: blogs cite each other back to an unpublished origin. The "75% auto-rejected" and "6/7.4-second scan" figures are the prime examples and are flagged in-line.
- Practitioner consensus (university career offices, professional resume writers, professional associations) is strong and internally consistent on structure, bullets, and tailoring, and is tagged [practitioner-consensus].
- Note: most evidence is US/UK general-market. Pharma/biotech-specific guidance below is [practitioner-consensus] from career-services sources, not empirical.

## 1. Section Structure and Order

- The dominant recommended order for senior/experienced professionals is: Contact -> Professional/Executive Summary -> Core Competencies (skills) -> Professional Experience -> Education -> optional sections (Certifications, Board/Affiliations, Publications). Experience leads the body; education moves below experience for experienced candidates. [practitioner-consensus] (ResumeSpice, Briefcase Coach, TopResume, Teal)
- Reverse-chronological is the strongly preferred format. Reporting commonly cites ~90% of recruiters preferring it; functional (skills-only) resumes are distrusted because they obscure timeline and recency and "feel like they're hiding something." [practitioner-consensus] (Jobscan, TopResume "death of the functional resume")
- A core-competencies / key-skills block placed high (just under the header or summary) is widely endorsed for senior resumes; it doubles as a keyword anchor and a fast-scan signal. [practitioner-consensus]
- Senior IC vs leadership: the section SET is largely the same; what changes is emphasis and content, not the skeleton. IC resumes foreground projects, systems, depth, and personal metrics; leadership resumes foreground scope, scale, and enterprise impact (revenue influenced, org size, budget). [practitioner-consensus] (thebiggamehunter.us, Resumly)
- A rigid universal order does NOT strictly hold; it varies by seniority (education position), sector (academic CV adds publications/grants prominently), and target (board roles add governance sections). The "summary -> competencies -> experience -> education -> certs" backbone is a strong default, not a law. [practitioner-consensus]
- LEGACY-CONVENTION CHECK: The proposed invariant 7-section order (Professional Summary, Core Competencies, Professional Experience, Selected Projects, Education, Certifications/Training, Technical Proficiencies) is well-aligned with practitioner consensus for senior roles. Caveats: (a) "Selected Projects" as a fixed standalone section is NOT a general convention; it is most justified for IC/technical and consulting profiles, and may be redundant with Experience for pure leadership resumes [low-confidence as an invariant]; (b) treating the order as strictly invariant across both IC and leadership is slightly stronger than the evidence: education placement and the projects/technical-proficiencies sections are the elements most likely to move or drop by profile.

## 2. AI / ATS Screening Reality

- MYTH (traced to origin): "75% of resumes are auto-rejected by ATS and never seen by a human." Traced to a sales pitch by Preptel, a resume-optimization vendor, circa 2012; the company folded in 2013 and no study/methodology was ever published. It propagated via an unverified Forbes citation (2014) -> CIO.com (2018) -> CNBC (2019) -> thousands of blogs. A Google Scholar search by Christine Assaf found zero academic support. Notably, vendor Jobscan still repeats "75% rejected" as of 2024, illustrating how entrenched the myth is. [empirical-debunk / the original number is fabricated]
- What actually happens: ATS organize, parse, sort, and rank applications against human-defined criteria; they generally do NOT autonomously reject. A recruiter survey reported ~92% of systems do not auto-reject on formatting/keywords/low match scores. The real screen-out is high-volume human triage. [practitioner-consensus; recruiter-survey, small/unspecified samples -> directional]
- Over-prescriptive criteria DO screen out qualified people, but humans set those filters. Harvard Business School + Accenture, "Hidden Workers: Untapped Talent" (2021): 88% of employers agreed qualified high-skill candidates get filtered out for not matching exact job-description criteria (94% for middle-skill); recruiting management systems filter/rank with highly prescriptive parameters (e.g., rigid degree requirements, employment-gap flags). [empirical] (large employer survey; the standout credible source for "good candidates get filtered")
- Parsing/formatting that measurably hurts: multi-column/table layouts (parsers read left-to-right, top-to-bottom and scramble columns), contact info in headers/footers (reportedly missed ~25% of the time), graphics/icons/skill-bars (invisible or rendered as null), nonstandard section headings, and exotic fonts. Single-column, standard headings, common fonts (Arial/Calibri/Times), .docx or text-based PDF are the safe defaults. [practitioner-consensus; the specific percentages (e.g., "70%+", "25%") are vendor-sourced and [low-confidence])
- Keyword/skill matching: using the job description's exact terminology for hard skills and technical terms helps both ATS ranking and human skim. Matching the posting's job title is repeatedly cited as a strong signal. The magnitude claims ("3.5x"/"10.6x more likely with exact title", "80%+ keyword match passes at 2.5x") are all Jobscan-internal and unpublished -> [low-confidence]; directionally consistent with practitioner consensus.
- The "6-second / 7.4-second recruiter scan": from Ladders eye-tracking studies (2012: 6s; 2018: 7.4s). This measures the INITIAL elimination skim only, not total review. More recent (vendor) data put initial scans nearer ~11s and full review of resumes that pass the threshold at 1.5-2.5+ minutes; ResumeGo similarly measured 2m24s (one-page) to 4m05s (two-page). Takeaway: design the top third for a fast skim AND provide depth for the longer read of qualified candidates. [empirical for the original eye-tracking; "longer read" figures are vendor-sourced -> directional]

## 3. Achievement / Bullet Construction

- Impact-first is the consensus. University career offices explicitly recommend leading with the result/impact, then how it was achieved. Yale's Office of Career Strategy gives two formulas: "action verb + project/problem solved + quantified result," and explicitly "Lead with the impact you delivered + numerically measure what you accomplished + detail what you did." [practitioner-consensus] (Yale OCS, Columbia Career Education, Western University)
- Quantify wherever possible. "Increased sales 20% in six months" beats "increased sales." NACE Job Outlook framing: employers want proof of problem-solving and results, best shown via measurable accomplishments rather than duty lists. [practitioner-consensus] (NACE-referenced; Yale; Jobscan examples)
- Accomplishment-framed bullets beat responsibility/duty-framed bullets across all reputable guidance. [practitioner-consensus]
- The CAR / STAR / CCAR family: all are accepted scaffolds for an accomplishment statement (Challenge/Context -> Action -> Result). On a resume bullet the Context/Challenge is compressed or implied and the Result is foregrounded; STAR is more often the spoken/interview form, CAR the written-bullet form. [practitioner-consensus]
- CCAR origin (useful provenance): Challenge-Context-Action-Result is the model mandated by the US Office of Personnel Management for writing Executive Core Qualifications (ECQs) for the Senior Executive Service. It is purpose-built for senior leadership narratives, which is why it maps well to director/leadership resumes and accomplishment statements. [empirical-provenance] (Resume Place, SES Writers, OPM-derived guidance)
- LEGACY-CONVENTION CHECK: Proposed CCAR (Challenge -> Strategy/Action -> Outcome), impact-first, is fully supported. One refinement from the evidence: on the resume itself, lead the bullet with the OUTCOME (impact-first), even though the underlying logical model is Challenge->Action->Result. So the storage model can be CCAR while the rendered bullet is result-first. Keep Context/Challenge terse on the page; reserve full CCAR narration for interviews/ECQ-style longform.

## 4. Professional Summary

- A summary/profile section IS recommended at senior level and should be the first content under the header; it functions as the value proposition / elevator pitch and primes both the human skim and keyword match. [practitioner-consensus] (ResumeSpice, Teal, Indeed, Dr. Kyle Elliott)
- Length guidance clusters at roughly 2-5 sentences, with executive summaries trending to the longer end (3-6 sentences) given depth of experience; some coaches note 2 sentences can signal confidence for very senior, focused candidates. There is no empirical optimum; this is practitioner judgment. [practitioner-consensus]
- Content for senior roles: strategic scope and leadership scale (team size, budget, P&L, cross-functional/enterprise reach), domain/industry positioning, and 1-2 quantified signature achievements, plus naturally woven keywords from the target posting (without keyword-stuffing a 30-40 term list). [practitioner-consensus]
- LEGACY-CONVENTION CHECK: Proposed 3-4 sentences sits squarely inside the consensus band and is a reasonable default. Mild caution: hold it as a guideline, not a hard rule. Executive profiles sometimes justify up to ~6 sentences; the 3-4 figure is [practitioner-consensus], not [empirical].

## 5. Employment Gaps and Career Transitions

- STRONGEST empirical finding in this whole brief: Kristal, Nicks, Gloor & Hauser (2022), Nature Human Behaviour. Field audit study, ~9,022 real UK job applications across eight sectors. Listing tenure as number of YEARS worked (e.g., "5 years") instead of date ranges increased callbacks by ~15% relative to resumes with visible (date-based) gaps, and ~8% even relative to resumes with no gap at all. Effect held for male and female applicants and across experience levels. [empirical]
- Implication: a "years of tenure" rendering option is an evidence-based design feature for handling gaps, and it appears to help even gap-free candidates by making experience more salient. (Trade-off: pure year-only formats can read as evasive to some recruiters and can confuse ATS date parsing; the study tested year-of-tenure framing, not removal of all chronology.)
- Discrimination against visible gaps is real and is worse for women with caregiving breaks. [empirical] (same literature / Nature Human Behaviour)
- Practitioner tactics that corroborate / extend this: omit months (use years only) for short gaps; add an explicit "Career Break" entry so both humans and ATS see a labeled reason; fill gaps with courses/projects/contract work. [practitioner-consensus] (CNBC, UW PCE, NCOA, FlexJobs)
- Career transitions / transferable skills: reverse-chronological remains preferred; functional resumes are distrusted by recruiters and parse poorly. The recommended vehicle is a HYBRID/combination resume: a strong summary plus a prominent skills/competencies block up top to surface transferable skills, followed by a standard chronological history for verifiability and ATS. Reframe accomplishments into the target domain's language. [practitioner-consensus] (TopResume, Jobscan, Teal)

## 6. Length Norms

- US "resume" convention: 1-2 pages historically, but empirical and practitioner evidence now favors two pages for experienced professionals. ResumeGo simulation (482 recruiters/hiring pros, 7,712 selections): recruiters were ~2.3x more likely to prefer two-page over one-page resumes overall, and the preference rose with seniority (entry ~1.4x, mid ~2.6x, managerial ~2.9x). Two-page resumes scored ~21% higher on quality. [empirical-ish: a single vendor simulation, methodology partially disclosed; the strongest length study available -> treat as directional-strong]
- Executive practitioner consensus: ~2 pages is the executive norm; 3 pages only when justified and trimmed of early-career detail. Dr. Kyle Elliott (executive coach; cited in HBR/Fortune/NYT): "the typical executive resume is two pages," >95% of his clients use two pages. [practitioner-consensus]
- "CV" (academic / international) vs US resume: a true academic CV is comprehensive and can run 3-10+ pages (publications, grants, teaching, presentations). In UK/EU/NZ "CV" simply means the resume-equivalent. So "CV" length norms depend entirely on which document is meant. [practitioner-consensus]
- LEGACY-CONVENTION CHECK: Proposed targets of 2-3 pages (IC) and 4-5 pages (leadership) are LONGER than the mainstream US-resume evidence supports. The empirical/practitioner center of gravity is ~2 pages, extending to 3 for very senior/deep histories. 4-5 pages is academic-CV / international-CV territory or a federal-resume exception, NOT standard industry director/leadership practice. CONTRADICTION FLAGGED: if these are industry (non-academic) targets, leadership at 4-5 pages risks reading as unfocused and over-long against recruiter expectations. Recommend recalibrating to roughly: senior IC 2 pages (3 if deep), leadership 2-3 pages, reserving 4+ pages only for genuine academic CVs, federal/SES resumes, or publication-heavy scientist profiles where the longer document is the expected norm. [practitioner-consensus + ResumeGo empirical]

## 7. Tailoring to a Specific Job Description

- Direction is clear and consistent: tailoring/requirement-mirroring to the JD improves callbacks. Multiple practitioner and vendor sources report large lifts. [practitioner-consensus on direction]
- The specific magnitudes are vendor-sourced and uncorroborated against peer-reviewed work: "50% more callbacks" (Jobscan), "40%" (Reztune), "60%" (Resume Now 2025), "3x" (Ladders 2024), "matching the job title -> 3.5x more likely to interview" (Jobscan, ~1M applications), "reorder experience -> 21% higher callback." These cluster directionally upward but no single figure is independently verified. [low-confidence on exact numbers; practitioner-consensus on direction]
- Mechanism that is well-grounded: tailoring (a) raises keyword/skill and job-title match for both ATS ranking and human skim, and (b) reorders the most relevant experience into the high-attention top third the eye-tracking data shows recruiters read first. These mechanisms are supported by the Ladders eye-tracking and Harvard Hidden-Workers (exact-criteria) findings even where the headline callback percentages are not. [empirical mechanism / low-confidence magnitude]
- Caveat: "tailoring" that degenerates into keyword-stuffing is warned against by practitioners and undermines the human read; tailoring should mean genuine relevance reordering and terminology alignment, not term dumping. [practitioner-consensus]

## Pharma / Biotech / Regulated-Industry Notes (cross-cutting)

- Use the sector's exact regulatory terminology as keywords AND as substance: GxP, GLP, GMP, GCP, GDP, FDA, ICH, IND, NDA, BLA, eCTD, clinical trial design, regulatory submissions, quality/compliance. [practitioner-consensus]
- But screening on acronym/keyword alone misses qualified life-sciences candidates; pair regulatory terms with demonstrated cross-functional collaboration and outcomes (quality improvements, submission timelines, audit/inspection results). Emphasize quantified outcomes over task lists, consistent with the general bullet guidance. [practitioner-consensus]
- Senior scientist / regulated-IC profiles are the case where a "Selected Projects"/technical-depth section and a longer (toward 3-page, or publication-listing) document are most defensible.
